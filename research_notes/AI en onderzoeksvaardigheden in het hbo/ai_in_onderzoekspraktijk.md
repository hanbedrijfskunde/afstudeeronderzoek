# How generative AI performs in the research tasks of business-administration students (evidence 2023–2026)

**Verification legend.** Every source below was checked on 18 Nov 2026 against the Crossref DOI registry (authors, title, venue, volume, issue, pages) unless noted. Key findings were taken from the version-of-record abstract (Crossref, Europe PMC or OpenAlex), from arXiv, or from the publisher/organisation page.
- **[PR]** peer-reviewed journal article or archival conference paper.
- **[PREPRINT]** not peer-reviewed (arXiv, SSRN, PsyArXiv, SocArXiv).
- **[GREY]** reports by NGOs, broadcasters, newsrooms or policy institutes.
- **[VENDOR]** a company's claims about its own or competing products.
- **UNVERIFIED** means a detail I could not confirm in a primary or registry source.

Proposals in the memo: **(1)** find 3 good sources yourself, then seed the AI with them; **(2)** hand-code first, then compare with AI coding; **(3)** learn how AI search and deep-research tools work and fail; **(4)** judge synthetic data and simulated respondents critically; **(5)** verify every AI claim against a source you have read.

## Key question 1: How often do LLMs and AI research tools fabricate or misattribute references, and has this improved in 2024–2026?

### Takeaway
Fabricated references have dropped but not disappeared. Plain chatbots without retrieval still fabricate about 20–40% of references, and many of the real ones contain errors (2023–2025 studies). Search-grounded tools and deep-research agents fabricate far fewer references, but the problem moves elsewhere. The cited source often exists but does not support the claim: 30% to over 90% of statements are unsupported, depending on the system. A residual 3–13% of cited URLs never existed. Specialised tools such as Elicit are precise but miss most relevant studies (sensitivity about 40%), and their results are not reproducible.

### Cited Findings

**A. Plain chatbots (no retrieval): fabricated and erroneous references**

- **Walters, W. H., & Wilder, E. I. (2023). Fabrication and errors in the bibliographic citations generated by ChatGPT. *Scientific Reports, 13*(1), 14045.** [PR; foundational] — [doi:10.1038/s41598-023-41032-5](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-023-41032-5)
  - *Design and sample:* GPT-3.5 and GPT-4 each wrote short literature reviews on 42 multidisciplinary topics, giving 84 papers with 636 citations. Each citation was checked in multiple databases.
  - *Findings:* **55% of GPT-3.5 citations and 18% of GPT-4 citations were fabricated.** Among the real citations, **43% (GPT-3.5) and 24% (GPT-4) contained substantive citation errors.**
  - *Limitations:* 2023 models with no web retrieval.
  - *Bears on:* (3), (5).
- **Bhattacharyya, M., Miller, V. M., Bhattacharyya, D., & Miller, L. E. (2023). High rates of fabricated and inaccurate references in ChatGPT-generated medical content. *Cureus*, e39238.** [PR; foundational] — [doi:10.7759/cureus.39238](https://doi.org/10.7759/cureus.39238)
  - Volume and issue are not listed in the Crossref record.
  - *Design and sample:* ChatGPT-3.5 wrote 30 short medical papers containing 115 references.
  - *Findings:* **47% of references were fabricated, 46% were authentic but inaccurate, and only 7% were authentic and accurate.** An incorrect PMID appeared in 93% of papers. On average a reference had 4.3 ± 2.8 of 7 elements wrong.
  - *Limitations:* single model, medical domain only, small sample.
  - *Bears on:* (5).
- **Chelli, M., Descamps, J., Lavoué, V., Trojani, C., Azar, M., Deckert, M., Raynier, J.-L., Clowez, G., Boileau, P., & Ruetsch-Chelli, C. (2024). Hallucination rates and reference accuracy of ChatGPT and Bard for systematic reviews: Comparative analysis. *Journal of Medical Internet Research, 26*, e53164.** [PR] — [doi:10.2196/53164](https://doi.org/10.2196/53164)
  - *Design and sample:* The models were asked to reproduce the reference lists of 11 human-conducted systematic reviews on rotator cuff pathology, given the same inclusion criteria. That gave 33 prompts and 471 references. A reference counted as "hallucinated" if any 2 of title, first author or year were wrong.
  - *Hallucination rates:* **GPT-3.5 39.6% (55/139), GPT-4 28.6% (34/119), Bard 91.4% (95/104).**
  - *Precision:* 9.4%, 13.4% and 0% respectively.
  - *Recall:* 11.9% (GPT-3.5), 13.7% (GPT-4), 0% (Bard).
  - *Further finding:* the retrieved papers showed geographic and open-access biases.
  - *Authors' conclusion:* LLMs should not be the primary or sole tool for systematic reviews, and every generated reference needs validation.
  - *Limitations:* one clinical field; models from 2023.
  - *Bears on:* (1), (3), (5).
- **Linardon, J., Jarman, H. K., McClure, Z., Anderson, C., Liu, C., & Messer, M. (2025). Influence of topic familiarity and prompt specificity on citation fabrication in mental health research using large language models: Experimental study. *JMIR Mental Health, 12*, e80371.** [PR] — [doi:10.2196/80371](https://doi.org/10.2196/80371); [article page](https://mental.jmir.org/2025/1/e80371)
  - *Design and sample:* In June 2025, GPT-4o wrote 6 literature reviews on 3 disorders that differ in public familiarity (major depressive disorder, binge-eating disorder, body dysmorphic disorder). Each disorder got a general and a specialised prompt. The reviews contained 176 citations.
  - *Findings:* **35 citations (19.9%) were fabricated, and 64 of the 141 real citations (45.4%) contained errors**, mostly wrong DOIs. Combined, nearly two-thirds of the citations were fabricated or inaccurate.
  - *Effect of topic and prompt:* fabrication was higher for less familiar topics. For depression, accuracy was higher with general prompts than with specialised ones (77% vs 50%, P = .03), as reported in the article.
  - *Per-disorder fabrication rates:* about 6% for depression vs about 28–29% for the less familiar disorders. These figures come from a secondary press summary ([GIGAZINE](https://www.gigazine.net/gsc_news/en/20251207-ai-generated-citations-fabrication-errors)); exact values are UNVERIFIED.
  - *Limitations:* one model, three topics, simple prompts.
  - *Bears on:* (3), (5).
  - *Point for students:* niche topics, which are typical of thesis work, carry a higher risk.
- **Cabezas-Clavijo, Á., & Sidorenko-Bautista, P. (2026). Assessing the performance of 8 AI chatbots in bibliographic reference retrieval: Grok and DeepSeek outperform ChatGPT, but none are entirely accurate. *Journal of Data and Information Science*.** [PR; published online April 2026; volume, issue and pages not yet in Crossref] — [doi:10.1515/jdis-2025-0326](https://doi.org/10.1515/jdis-2025-0326); preprint [arXiv:2505.18059](https://arxiv.org/abs/2505.18059)
  - *Design and sample:* free versions of 8 chatbots (ChatGPT, Claude, Copilot, DeepSeek, Gemini, Grok, Le Chat, Perplexity) with a standardised prompt. 400 references across 5 broad disciplines, including Social Sciences.
  - *Findings:* **only 26.5% of references were fully correct, 33.8% were partially correct, and 39.8% were erroneous or entirely fabricated.** Grok and DeepSeek generated no false references. Copilot, Perplexity and Claude had the highest hallucination rates.
  - *Limitations:* free tiers only, one prompt; the testing date is not stated on the arXiv page.
  - *Bears on:* (3), (5).

**B. Search-grounded answers and deep-research agents: do the cited sources support the claims?**

- **Wu, K., Wu, E., Wei, K., Zhang, A., Casasola, A., Nguyen, T., Riantawan, S., Shi, P., Ho, D., & Zou, J. (2025). An automated framework for assessing how well LLMs cite relevant medical references. *Nature Communications, 16*(1), 3615.** [PR] — [doi:10.1038/s41467-025-58551-6](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41467-025-58551-6)
  - *Design and sample:* SourceCheckup, an automated pipeline validated by doctors. 7 LLMs, 800 medical questions, 58,000 statement–source pairs.
  - *Findings:* **50–90% of LLM responses were not fully supported by the sources they cite, and some were contradicted by them.** Even **GPT-4o with Web Search left about 30% of individual statements unsupported, and nearly half of its responses were not fully supported.**
  - *Limitations:* medical questions only; models from 2024.
  - *Bears on:* (5), (3).
  - *Key point:* a source can be real and still not say what the AI attributes to it.
- **Venkit, P. N., Laban, P., Zhou, Y., Huang, K.-H., Mao, Y., & Wu, C.-S. (2025). DeepTRACE: Auditing deep research AI systems for tracking reliability across citations and evidence. arXiv:2509.04499.** [PREPRINT; Salesforce AI Research team] — [arXiv:2509.04499](https://arxiv.org/abs/2509.04499); [HTML with tables](https://arxiv.org/html/2509.04499)
  - *Design and sample:* 303 queries (168 debate questions and 135 expertise questions) across 9 systems in search mode and deep-research mode. Eight audit dimensions. Scoring by an LLM judge validated against human raters.
  - *Search mode (Table 1):* unsupported statements were 47.0% for GPT-4.5, 30.8% for You.com, 31.6% for Perplexity and 23.1% for Bing Copilot. Citation accuracy was 39.8%, 68.3%, 49.0% and 65.8% respectively. One-sided answers to debate questions were 90.4% for GPT-4.5 and 83.4% for Perplexity.
  - *Deep-research mode:*

    | System | Unsupported statements | Citation accuracy |
    |---|---|---|
    | GPT-5 Deep Research | **12.5%** | **79.1%** |
    | YouChat DR | 74.6% | 72.3% |
    | Perplexity DR | **97.5%** | 58.0% |
    | Copilot Think Deeper | 90.2% | 62.1% |
    | Gemini DR | 53.6% | 50.3% |

  - *Inconsistency in the paper:* the text gives Gemini DR's citation accuracy as 40.3%, while Table 1 gives 50.3%.
  - *Limitations:* preprint; the systems are moving targets.
  - *Bears on:* (3), (5).
  - *Key point:* systems differ enormously, and "deep research" does not mean the output is supported by sources.
- **Rao, D., Wong, E., & Callison-Burch, C. (2026). Detecting and correcting reference hallucinations in commercial LLMs and deep research agents. arXiv:2604.03173.** [PREPRINT; University of Pennsylvania] — [arXiv:2604.03173](https://arxiv.org/abs/2604.03173)
  - *Design and sample:* DRBench (10 models and agents, 53,090 URLs) and ExpertQA (3 models, 168,021 URLs across 32 academic fields).
  - *Findings:* **3–13% of citation URLs have no record in the Wayback Machine and "likely never existed"**, and 5–18% of URLs do not resolve. Deep-research agents produce more citations per query but **hallucinate URLs at higher rates** than search-augmented LLMs. Non-resolving rates were lowest in Business (5.4%) and highest in Theology (11.4%).
  - *Correction tool:* their open-source checker, urlhealth, cut non-resolving URLs by 6–79×, to below 1%. It catches dead or invented links but not unsupported claims.
  - *Bears on:* (3), (5).

**C. Specialised academic AI search tools (Elicit and others)**

- **Lau, O., & Golder, S. (2025). Comparison of Elicit AI and traditional literature searching in evidence syntheses using four case studies. *Cochrane Evidence Synthesis and Methods, 3*(6), e70050.** [PR] — [doi:10.1002/cesm.70050](https://doi.org/10.1002/cesm.70050)
  - *Design and sample:* Elicit Pro in Review mode compared against the included studies of 4 evidence syntheses.
  - *Sensitivity:* **Elicit averaged 39.5% (range 25.5–69.2%), against 94.5% (91.1–98.0%) for the original searches.**
  - *Precision:* **Elicit averaged 41.8% (35.6–46.2%), against 7.55% (0.65–14.7%) for the original searches.**
  - *Unique studies:* Elicit found some included studies that the original searches had missed.
  - *Conclusion:* not sensitive enough to replace traditional searching, but useful for preliminary searches and as an adjunct.
  - *Source discrepancy:* an earlier search summary gave 37.9% sensitivity and 39.6% precision (probably the medRxiv preprint version). The figures above are from the version of record.
  - *Bears on:* (1), (3).
- **Bernard, N., Sagawa Jr, Y., Bier, N., Lihoreau, T., Pazart, L., & Tannou, T. (2025). Using artificial intelligence for systematic review: The example of Elicit. *BMC Medical Research Methodology, 25*(1), 75.** [PR] — [doi:10.1186/s12874-025-02528-y](https://doi.org/10.1186/s12874-025-02528-y)
  - *Design:* an umbrella review conducted without AI was compared with Elicit searches using the same criteria, and the Elicit search was repeated 3 times.
  - *Repeatability:* **three identical runs returned 246, 169 and 172 results.**
  - *Accuracy:* 3 articles were found by both, 3 only by Elicit, and **17 only by the traditional search.**
  - *Conclusion:* a valuable complementary tool, to be used with caution.
  - *Bears on:* (1), (3).
- **Park, S. G. (2026). AI and systematic reviews: Can AI tools replace librarians in the systematic search process? *Science & Technology Libraries, 45*(2), 131–151.** [PR; qualitative tool review] — [doi:10.1080/0194262x.2025.2521519](https://doi.org/10.1080/0194262x.2025.2521519)
  - Reviews AI-based search tools for systematic reviews and raises transparency, reproducibility and trustworthiness issues.
  - No pooled performance metrics in the abstract.
  - *Bears on:* (3).
- **Bolaños, F., Salatino, A., Osborne, F., & Motta, E. (2024). Artificial intelligence for literature reviews: Opportunities and challenges. *Artificial Intelligence Review, 57*(10), 259.** [PR; review] — [doi:10.1007/s10462-024-10902-3](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10462-024-10902-3)
  - Examines 21 SLR tools (23 traditional and 11 AI features) and 11 LLM-based search and writing tools.
  - Names the lack of a **standardised evaluation framework** as a key challenge.
  - Useful for explaining how the tools work (screening and extraction stages).
  - *Bears on:* (3).
- **Consensus, Scite, ResearchRabbit:** I found **no independent peer-reviewed accuracy study**. A 200-query benchmark published by SciSpace, a competing vendor, puts Consensus and Elicit average precision at about 0.31–0.35 ([SciSpace, VENDOR](https://scispace.com/resources/scispace-vs-elicit-vs-consensus-an-ai-literature-search-benchmark-across-200-queries/)). It is not usable as evidence.

**D. AI search engines in general (grey literature, large samples)**

- **Jaźwińska, K., & Chandrasekar, A. (2025, March 6). AI search has a citation problem. *Columbia Journalism Review*, Tow Center for Digital Journalism.** [GREY; journalism research] — [cjr.org](https://www.cjr.org/tow_center/we-compared-eight-ai-search-engines-theyre-all-bad-at-citing-news.php)
  - *Design:* 8 tools; 1,600 queries (20 publishers × 10 articles × 8 tools) asking each tool to identify a news article's headline, publisher, date and URL from an excerpt; tested February 2025.
  - *Overall:* the tools were **wrong on more than 60% of queries.** **Perplexity was wrong on 37% and Grok 3 on 94%.** ChatGPT Search misidentified 134 of 200 articles and signalled uncertainty only 15 times.
  - *Paid tiers:* answered more queries correctly but also had higher error rates, because they gave confident wrong answers instead of declining.
  - *Links:* more than half of Gemini's and Grok 3's responses cited fabricated or broken URLs; for Grok 3, 154 of 200 links led to error pages.
  - *Limitations:* news, not scholarly sources; one run per excerpt.
  - *Bears on:* (3), (5).
- **European Broadcasting Union & BBC (2025, October). *News Integrity in AI Assistants*.** [GREY] — [EBU press release](https://www.ebu.ch/news/2025/10/ai-s-systemic-distortion-of-news-is-consistent-across-languages-and-territories-international-study-by-public-service-broadcaste)
  - *Design:* public-service journalists in several countries rated more than 3,000 responses from ChatGPT, Copilot, Gemini and Perplexity.
  - *Findings:* **45% of answers had at least one significant issue, 31% had serious sourcing problems** (missing, misleading or incorrect attribution), and 20% had major accuracy issues. **Gemini had significant issues in 76% of answers.**
  - *Bears on:* (3).

**G. User-side failure modes (how people use AI search)**

- **Spatharioti, S. E., Rothschild, D., Goldstein, D. G., & Hofman, J. M. (2025). Effects of LLM-based search on decision making: Speed, accuracy, and overreliance. ### Inferences
- **Trend 2023 → 2025/26.** For plain chatbots the fabrication rate fell from 55% (GPT-3.5) to 18–20% (GPT-4 in 2023, GPT-4o in 2025: Walters & Wilder; Linardon et al.). The share of real-but-erroneous references stayed high (24–45%). Across 8 free chatbots in 2025, 40% of references were still wrong or fabricated (Cabezas-Clavijo & Sidorenko-Bautista). There is no evidence that the problem has been solved.
- **Retrieval moves the error.** Search-grounded and deep-research tools fabricate fewer references, but 30–97% of their statements can be unsupported by the cited source (Wu et al.; DeepTRACE), and 3–13% of URLs are invented (Rao et al.). This directly supports proposal **(5)**: checking that a reference exists is not enough; students must check that it supports the claim. That requires reading the source.
- **Large differences between tools** (GPT-5 Deep Research at 12.5% vs Perplexity DR at 97.5% unsupported statements) support proposal **(3)**. Students should learn that "AI search" is not one thing, and that performance changes from one version to the next.
- **Elicit-type tools** have high precision but low recall (Lau & Golder) and are not reproducible (Bernard et al.). This supports positioning AI as an **adjunct** to an initial human search, as in proposal **(1)**, not a replacement. It also conflicts with the transparency and reproducibility norms of a methods section.
- **Risk for business students:** less-familiar or niche topics show higher fabrication (Linardon), and many business theses are niche, company-specific topics.

### Gaps
- No peer-reviewed independent evaluation of **Consensus, Scite, ResearchRabbit, Gemini Deep Research or Perplexity Deep Research specifically on scholarly literature reviews** in business or management was found. Evidence is mostly from medicine, news or general QA.
- No study measures reference fabrication on Dutch-language or Dutch business topics.
- DeepTRACE and Rao et al. are preprints; tool versions change every few months, so any figure will age quickly.
- OpenAI's own limitation statement could not be fetched (HTTP 403); the wording is UNVERIFIED.

## Key question 2: How reliable is LLM-assisted qualitative coding compared with human coders, and which workflows do methodologists recommend?

### Takeaway
On **deductive** coding with a clear codebook, LLMs reach "moderate to substantial" agreement with humans: Cohen's κ is typically about 0.6–0.8 overall. Some individual codes fall as low as κ 0.13–0.28, and agreement drops for interpretive, latent or time-dependent codes. On **inductive** thematic analysis, LLMs recover most descriptive themes but miss subtle interpretive ones, and coding agreement for shared themes can be only 36–47%. Methodologists converge on human-led workflows: build the codebook first, rewrite definitions for the model, validate against human coding, have the model explain its decisions, and review every output. A large group of reflexive-TA scholars (419 signatories) rejects GenAI for reflexive approaches altogether, and a published reply contests that.

### Cited Findings

**A. Foundational annotation studies (2023–2024)**

- **Gilardi, F., Alizadeh, M., & Kubli, M. (2023). ChatGPT outperforms crowd workers for text-annotation tasks. *PNAS, 120*(30), e2305016120.** [PR; foundational] — [doi:10.1073/pnas.2305016120](https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.2305016120)
  - *Sample:* 4 datasets of tweets and news articles (n = 6,183).
  - *Findings:* ChatGPT's zero-shot accuracy exceeded crowd workers' by **about 25 percentage points on average**, and its intercoder agreement exceeded both crowd workers and trained annotators on all tasks. Cost was below $0.003 per annotation.
  - *Limitations:* simple classification tasks (relevance, stance, topic, frame), not interview coding.
  - *Bears on:* (2).
- **Ziems, C., Held, W., Shaikh, O., Chen, J., Zhang, Z., & Yang, D. (2024). Can large language models transform computational social science? *Computational Linguistics, 50*(1), 237–291.** [PR] — [doi:10.1162/coli_a_00502](https://doi.org/10.1162/coli_a_00502)
  - *Design:* 13 LLMs on 25 English computational-social-science benchmarks.
  - *Findings:* on classification, LLMs **do not beat the best fine-tuned models but reach "fair" agreement with humans.** On free-form explanation tasks they often exceed crowdworkers' gold references.
  - *Recommendation:* use LLMs as a zero-shot annotator *within* human teams.
  - *Bears on:* (2).
- **Törnberg, P. (2024). Best practices for text annotation with large language models. *Sociologica, 18*(2), 67–85.** [PR; University of Amsterdam] — DOI 10.6092/issn.1971-8853/19461 per the [journal page](https://sociologica.unibo.it/article/view/19461) and DOAJ
  - The Crossref API returned 404 for this DOI; metadata were verified from the journal and DOAJ listings.
  - Guidelines cover model selection, prompt engineering, structured prompting, **prompt stability analysis, model validation against human coding**, and ethical and legal issues.
  - *Bears on:* (2).

**B. Deductive coding (codebook given): agreement with humans**

- **Xiao, Z., Yuan, X., Liao, Q. V., Abdelghani, R., & Oudeyer, P.-Y. (2023). Supporting qualitative analysis with large language models: Combining codebook with GPT-3 for deductive coding. In *Companion Proceedings of the 28th International Conference on Intelligent User Interfaces (IUI '23)* (pp. 75–78). ACM.** [PR; short paper] — [doi:10.1145/3581754.3584136](https://doi.org/10.1145/3581754.3584136)
  - GPT-3 combined with an expert-drafted codebook reached **"fair to substantial" agreement** with expert coding on curiosity-driven questions.
  - *Bears on:* (2).
- **Tai, R. H., Bentley, L. R., Xia, X., Sitt, J. M., Fankhauser, S. C., Chicas-Mosier, A. M., & Monteith, B. G. (2024). An examination of the use of large language models to aid analysis of textual data. *International Journal of Qualitative Methods, 23*.** [PR] — [doi:10.1177/16094069241231168](https://doi.org/10.1177/16094069241231168)
  - *Design:* 3 interview excerpts plus a codebook. The LLM was asked whether each code was present **and to give supporting evidence**. Each excerpt was run **160 times**, treating each run as a new coder, and compared with human coders.
  - *Conclusion:* LLMs can provide a "systematic and reliable platform" for deductive code identification.
  - *Limitations:* very small text sample.
  - *Bears on:* (2).
- **Bijker, R., Merkouris, S. S., Dowling, N. A., & Rodda, S. N. (2024). ChatGPT for automated qualitative research: Content analysis. *Journal of Medical Internet Research, 26*, e59050.** [PR] — [doi:10.2196/59050](https://doi.org/10.2196/59050)
  - *Design:* 537 forum posts; inductive and deductive (Theoretical Domains Framework) content analysis; 10 coding schemes applied in 10 fresh conversations each.
  - *Precision:* detecting change mechanisms reached **66–88%**.
  - *Overall κ:* **0.72–0.82 for inductive schemes; 0.58–0.73 for deductive schemes.**
  - *Category-level κ:* **0.67–0.95 inductive vs 0.13–0.87 deductive.**
  - *Conclusion:* "fairly reliable", and better at building an inductive scheme than at adapting an existing framework.
  - *Bears on:* (2): theory-driven (deductive) codes can be very unreliable for individual categories.
- **Balt, E., Salmi, S., Bhulai, S., Vrinzen, S., Eikelenboom, M., Gilissen, R., Creemers, D., Popma, A., & Mérelle, S. (2025). Deductively coding psychosocial autopsy interview data using a few-shot learning large language model. *Frontiers in Public Health, 13*, 1512537.** [PR; **Dutch study**: 113 Suicide Prevention, Amsterdam UMC, VU and others] — [doi:10.3389/fpubh.2025.1512537](https://doi.org/10.3389/fpubh.2025.1512537)
  - *Design and sample:* 38 two-hour **Dutch-language** interviews. Llama 3 70B was run **offline** (privacy). The few-shot prompt gave one positive and one negative example per code. The gold standard was two independent human coders plus third-researcher consensus.
  - *Binary classification:* accuracy 0.84, **κ = 0.68 (substantial)**, with **per-code κ ranging from 0.24 to 1.00**.
  - *Sliding-window segment detection:* accuracy 0.67.
  - *Summaries:* about 80% were rated good or adequate. Errors included unsolicited elaboration in at least 19.7% of segments and hallucinations in at least 1.3% of summaries. The model performed poorly on time-dependent codes.
  - *Speed:* about 5 hours per interview, against about 4 months for the researchers on all 38 interviews.
  - *Recommendations:* a collaborative model in which the LLM does a first-pass deductive coding and humans review, interpret and do the inductive coding; verify all output; split broad codes into mutually exclusive subcodes and avoid negation-based definitions; build an active-learning feedback loop.
  - *Limitations:* one model, one dataset, no separate test set.
  - *Bears on:* (2). Highly relevant for HAN: Dutch-language data, local model, explicit human-in-the-loop.

**C. Inductive or thematic analysis: overlap with human themes**

- **Morgan, D. L. (2023). Exploring the use of artificial intelligence for qualitative data analysis: The case of ChatGPT. *International Journal of Qualitative Methods, 22*.** [PR] — [doi:10.1177/16094069231211248](https://doi.org/10.1177/16094069231211248)
  - Re-analysed 2 previously coded datasets.
  - ChatGPT was **better at concrete, descriptive themes and weaker at subtle, interpretive themes**, and was easy and fast to use.
  - *Bears on:* (2).
- **Hamilton, L., Elliott, D., Quick, A., Smith, S., & Choplin, V. (2023). Exploring the use of AI in qualitative analysis: A comparative study of guaranteed income data. *International Journal of Qualitative Methods, 22*.** [PR] — [doi:10.1177/16094069231201504](https://doi.org/10.1177/16094069231201504)
  - **Human coders found themes ChatGPT missed, and vice versa.**
  - The authors suggest using AI themes in **triangulation discussions** to spot oversights and biases.
  - *Bears on:* (2): the "explain the differences" step.
- **De Paoli, S. (2024). Performing an inductive thematic analysis of semi-structured interviews with a large language model: An exploration and provocation on the limits of the approach. *Social Science Computer Review, 42*(4), 997–1019.** [PR] — [doi:10.1177/08944393231220483](https://doi.org/10.1177/08944393231220483)
  - GPT-3.5-Turbo was run through Braun & Clarke's 6 phases on 2 open interview datasets.
  - It inferred **most of the main themes** of the original analyses. Only parts of the TA process can be reproduced.
  - *Bears on:* (2).
- **Prescott, M. R., Yeager, S., Ham, L., Rivera Saldana, C. D., Serrano, V., Narez, J., Paltin, D., Delgado, J., Moore, D. J., & Montoya, J. (2024). Comparing the efficacy and efficiency of human and generative AI: Qualitative thematic analyses. *JMIR AI, 3*, e54482.** [PR] — [doi:10.2196/54482](https://doi.org/10.2196/54482)
  - *Design and sample:* 40 SMS texts; two human teams compared with ChatGPT and Bard.
  - *Theme consistency:* **71% (5/7) of themes matched under inductive analysis, but only 50% (ChatGPT) and 58% (Bard) under deductive analysis.**
  - *Coding agreement on shared themes:* **only 36–47%** (ChatGPT 47% inductive, 37% deductive; Bard 37% inductive, 36% deductive).
  - *Time:* **20 min vs 567 min.**
  - *Limitations:* very short texts.
  - *Bears on:* (2).
- **Mathis, W. S., Zhao, S., Pratt, N., Weleff, J., & De Paoli, S. (2024). Inductive thematic analysis of healthcare qualitative interviews using open-source large language models: How does it compare to traditional methods? *Computer Methods and Programs in Biomedicine, 255*, 108356.** [PR] — [doi:10.1016/j.cmpb.2024.108356](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cmpb.2024.108356)
  - A local open-source 70B model on real psychiatric interviews containing protected health information.
  - Theme similarity to human analysis: **Jaccard 0.44–0.69 (moderate to substantial).**
  - *Bears on:* (2), and privacy for company data.
- **Wachinger, J., Bärnighausen, K., Schäfer, L. N., Scott, K., & McMahon, S. A. (2025). Prompts, pearls, imperfections: Comparing ChatGPT and a human researcher in qualitative data analysis. *Qualitative Health Research, 35*(9), 951–966.** [PR] — [doi:10.1177/10497323241244669](https://doi.org/10.1177/10497323241244669)
  - One interview transcript, analysed with various prompts by ChatGPT and by an experienced researcher.
  - The themes **overlapped considerably**. ChatGPT leaned descriptive but found some nuanced themes.
  - **When asked to embed the findings in theory, it "could convincingly argue how identified themes linked to the provided theories, even in cases of (seemingly) unfitting models."**
  - *Bears on:* (2), (5). Plausible theoretical framing is not evidence.
- **Bennis, I., & Mouwafaq, S. (2025). Advancing AI-driven thematic analysis in qualitative research: A comparative study of nine generative models on Cutaneous Leishmaniasis data. *BMC Medical Informatics and Decision Making, 25*(1), 124.** [PR] — [doi:10.1186/s12911-025-02961-5](https://doi.org/10.1186/s12911-025-02961-5)
  - 448 participant responses; 9 models (including Claude 3.5 Sonnet, ChatGPT o1-Pro, Gemini 2.0, DeepSeek V3) compared with expert analysis using Cohen's κ and Jaccard.
  - Some models reached **Jaccard = 1.00**.
  - *Limitations:* one dataset of short responses; very optimistic results. Treat with caution.
  - *Bears on:* (2).
- **Than, N., Fan, L., Law, T., Nelson, L. K., & McCall, L. (2025). Updating "The Future of Coding": Qualitative coding with generative large language models. *Sociological Methods & Research, 54*(3), 849–888.** [PR] — [doi:10.1177/00491241251339188](https://doi.org/10.1177/00491241251339188)
  - Tested multiple prompt structures on 4 open- and closed-source LLMs.

**D. Workflow and methodological guidance (2024–2026)**

- **Dunivin, Z. O. (2025). Scaling hermeneutics: A guide to qualitative coding with LLMs for reflexive content analysis. *EPJ Data Science, 14*(1), 28.** [PR] — [doi:10.1140/epjds/s13688-025-00548-8](https://doi.org/10.1140/epjds/s13688-025-00548-8)
  - The workflow **keeps the traditional human cycle of codebook development and refinement**, then adds a step that rewrites code definitions for machine comprehension before automating categorisation.

- **Nguyen-Trung, K. (2025). ChatGPT in thematic analysis: Can AI become a research assistant in qualitative research? *Quality & Quantity, 59*(6), 4945–4978.** [PR] — [doi:10.1007/s11135-025-02165-z](https://doi.org/10.1007/s11135-025-02165-z)

  - *Bears on:* (2).
- **Naeem, M., Smith, T., & Thomas, L. (2025).   - *Bears on:* (2).
- **Hitch, D. (2024). Artificial intelligence augmented qualitative analysis: The way of the future? *Qualitative Health Research, 34*(7), 595–606.** [PR; worked example] — [doi:10.1177/10497323231217392](https://doi.org/10.1177/10497323231217392)
  - Worked example of ChatGPT *augmenting*, not replacing, reflexive TA.
  - *Bears on:* (2).
- **Lee, V. V., van der Lubbe, S. C. C., Goh, L. H., & Valderas, J. M. (2024). Harnessing ChatGPT for thematic analysis: Are we ready? *Journal of Medical Internet Research, 26*, e54974.** [PR; viewpoint] — [doi:10.2196/54974](https://doi.org/10.2196/54974)

  - Human intervention remains necessary.
  - *Bears on:* (2).
- **Schroeder, H., Aubin Le Quéré, M., Randazzo, C., Mimno, D., & Schoenebeck, S. (2025). Large language models in qualitative research: Uses, tensions, and intentions.   - *Bears on:* (2).

**E. Principled rejection and the debate that followed (2025–2026)**

- **Jowsey, T., Braun, V., Clarke, V., Lupton, D., & Fine, M. (2026). We reject the use of generative artificial intelligence for reflexive qualitative research. *Qualitative Inquiry, 32*(10), 931–935.** [PR; open letter, online first 2025; also SSRN 10.2139/ssrn.5676462] — [doi:10.1177/10778004251401851](https://doi.org/10.1177/10778004251401851)
  - **419 experienced qualitative researchers from 32 countries** signed.
  - Their position: reflexive TA and similar approaches require "a subjective, positioned, and reflexive researcher", so GenAI is **"not methodologically congruent"**. They also reject it on social and environmental justice grounds.
  - Scope: "Big Q" reflexive approaches, not all qualitative coding.
  - *Bears on:* (2): contradicts using AI for reflexive TA. It is compatible with hand-coding first.
- **Friese, S., Nguyen-Trung, K., Powell, S., & Morgan, D. L. (2026). Beyond binary positions: Making space for critical and reflexive GenAI integration in qualitative research. *Qualitative Inquiry, 32*(10), 939–948.** [PR; response] — [doi:10.1177/10778004261429393](https://doi.org/10.1177/10778004261429393)
  - Argues that a categorical rejection "risks closing off methodological evolution".
  - GenAI "when used critically and under close researcher leadership and control" can be a legitimate analytic support, comparable to CAQDAS.
  - *Bears on:* (2).

### Inferences
- **Proposal (2) is well supported by method guidance.** Every workflow paper (Dunivin; Than et al.; Balt et al.; Nguyen-Trung; Törnberg) puts human codebook development and human validation before or around LLM coding. Comparing human and AI codes is the standard validation step (κ, Jaccard, theme overlap). Hamilton et al. and Lee et al. explicitly frame disagreements as material for triangulation, which is what "explain the differences" asks students to do.
- **What students should expect.** Moderate to substantial overall κ hides very poor agreement on some codes (κ 0.13–0.28 in Bijker; Balt). AI is reliable for descriptive codes and unreliable for latent or interpretive ones (Morgan; Prescott). AI will make even unfitting theories sound plausible (Wachinger). These make good, concrete learning targets for the comparison exercise.
- **Deductive vs inductive.** The pattern is mixed: Bijker et al. found inductive better than deductive, while Prescott et al. found theme consistency lower for deductive analysis. A clear, mutually exclusive codebook appears to matter more than the mode (Balt; Dunivin).
- **Privacy.** Interview data from companies often cannot go into cloud chatbots. Balt et al. and Mathis et al. show that local open-weight models (Llama 70B) can reach substantial agreement, which is relevant if HAN offers an institutional or local option.
- **Reflexive TA.** If the curriculum teaches Braun & Clarke's reflexive TA, the Jowsey et al. letter is a serious counter-position. AI comparison then fits better with codebook or template approaches (Nguyen-Trung; Dunivin) than with reflexive TA.

### Gaps
- No study found that runs the exact didactic design (students hand-code first, then compare with AI) and measures **learning outcomes**. All the studies are about research quality, not learning.
- Almost no data on **Dutch-language** coding beyond Balt et al. (2025), and none on business or organisational interviews specifically.
- Few studies report **human–human κ alongside human–AI κ** on the same data (Balt et al. did not report it), which makes benchmarking difficult.
- Several 2026 arXiv preprints argue that agreement with human coders is not the same as coding quality; I did not verify them, so they are not included.
- Hayes (2025), "'Conversing' with qualitative data" (*IJQM*, 24, [doi:10.1177/16094069251322346](https://doi.org/10.1177/16094069251322346)), was verified in Crossref, but its findings were not reviewed.

## Key question 3: Can "silicon samples" or synthetic respondents replace survey or interview participants?

### Takeaway
At the **aggregate** level, LLMs can approximate average opinions and predict the direction of experimental effects. GPT-4 predictions of 70 U.S. survey experiments correlated strongly with actual effects, but **overestimated effect sizes**. At the **distribution and inference** level, synthetic samples fail repeatedly: variance is too low, regression coefficients are biased, results shift with prompt wording and with time, minority groups are flattened or misportrayed, and responses are skewed outside the U.S. (Germany). Marketing scholars broadly recommend synthetic respondents only for **upstream** work such as pretesting, pilots and hypothesis generation, always with human validation. A new threat runs the other way: AI agents now pass 99.8% of attention checks, so "human" online panels may themselves contain synthetic data.

**A. Foundational optimistic and critical studies (political science)**

  - *Bears on:* (4).
- **Bisbee, J., Clinton, J. D., Dorff, C., Kenkel, B., & Larson, J. M. (2024). Synthetic replacements for human survey data? The perils of large language models. *Political Analysis, 32*(4), 401–416.** [PR] — [doi:10.1017/pan.2024.5](https://doi.org/10.1017/pan.2024.5)
  - *Design:* ChatGPT persona responses to feeling thermometers for 11 groups, compared with ANES 2016–2020.
  - **Averages matched closely, but the samples are "not reliable for statistical inference":** there is less variation than in real surveys, and regression coefficients often differ significantly.

  - *Bears on:* (4).
- **Dominguez-Olmedo, R., Hardt, M., & Mendler-Dünner, C. (2024). Questioning the survey responses of large language models. In *Advances in Neural Information Processing Systems 37 (NeurIPS 2024)* (pp. 45850–45878).** [PR] — [doi:10.52202/079017-1458](https://doi.org/10.52202/079017-1458)
  - 43 LLMs on the American Community Survey.
  - Responses are dominated by **ordering and labelling biases** (for example, toward option "A").
  - When answer order is randomised, models **"trend towards uniformly random survey responses"**, regardless of size.
  - *Bears on:* (4).
- **Boelaert, J., Coavoux, S., Ollion, É., Petev, I., & Präg, P. (2025). Machine bias: How do generative language models answer opinion polls? *Sociological Methods & Research, 54*(3), 1156–1196.** [PR] — [doi:10.1177/00491241251330582](https://doi.org/10.1177/00491241251330582)
  - Models **"cannot replace research subjects for opinion or attitudinal research"**.
  - They show **strong bias and low variance** on each topic, and the bias **varies randomly from topic to topic**. The authors call this "machine bias".
  - *Bears on:* (4).
- **von der Heyde, L., Haensch, A.-C., & Wenz, A. (2026). Vox populi, vox AI? Using large language models to estimate German vote choice. *Social Science Computer Review, 44*(3), 549–571.** [PR; European context] — [doi:10.1177/08944393251337014](https://doi.org/10.1177/08944393251337014)
  - A synthetic sample matching German Longitudinal Election Study 2017 respondents; GPT-3.5 predicted each respondent's vote.
  - **Bias toward the Greens and the Left.** The model captures "typical" voters but misses the more complex factors behind vote choice.
  - U.S.-based validity does not transfer to non-English contexts.
  - *Bears on:* (4). Relevant for Dutch students: findings validated on U.S. data may not hold in NL.
- **Wang, A., Morgenstern, J., & Dickerson, J. P. (2025). Large language models that replace human participants can harmfully misportray and flatten identity groups. *Nature Machine Intelligence, 7*(3), 400–411.** [PR] — [doi:10.1038/s42256-025-00986-z](https://doi.org/10.1038/s42256-025-00986-z)
  - Human studies with **3,200 participants across 16 demographic identities**, tested on 4 LLMs (per the [arXiv abstract](https://arxiv.org/abs/2402.01908)).
  - LLMs **misportray** groups (reflecting out-group views of a group, not in-group views) and **flatten** within-group diversity.
  - Inference-time fixes reduce but do not eliminate these harms.
  - *Bears on:* (4).

**B. Higher-fidelity approaches (2024–2026)**

- **Ashokkumar, A., Hewitt, L., Ghezae, I., & Willer, R. (2026). Large language models can predict the results of social science experiments. *Nature, 656*(8126), 115–122.** [PR; published 8 July 2026; preprint versions 2024–2025] — [doi:10.1038/s41586-026-10742-x](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41586-026-10742-x); abstract via [Stanford GSB](https://www.gsb.stanford.edu/faculty-research/publications/large-language-models-can-predict-results-social-science-experiments)
  - *Design and sample:* 70 preregistered, nationally representative U.S. survey experiments with 469 effects and 119,330 participants.
  - GPT-4-derived predictions were **"strongly correlated with actual treatment effects"**, similar in accuracy to pooled human forecasts. The preprint reported r = 0.85 ([Stanford HAI](https://hai.stanford.edu/news/social-science-moves-in-silico)); the Nature figure is UNVERIFIED.
  - However, **"predictions systematically overestimated effect sizes."** On 15 megastudies (606 effects), correlations were lower.
  - Secondary coverage reports some accuracy differences favouring white and Republican samples ([QUT commentary](https://www.qut.edu.au/news/realfocus/ai-can-predict-how-youll-respond-to-a-survey.-but-thats-not-the-same-as-understanding-you)).
  - *Limitations:* U.S., text-based survey experiments. A co-author warns against using simulation to *replace* human experiments.
  - *Bears on:* (4). This is the strongest pro-evidence, and it supports use for prediction and pilots, not for replacing participants.
- **Park, J. S., Zou, C. Q., Kamphorst, J., Egan, N., Shaw, A., Hill, B. M., Cai, C., Morris, M. R., Liang, P., Willer, R., & Bernstein, M. S. (2024–2026). AI simulations grounded in rich self-reports increase accuracy and reduce distortion. arXiv:2411.10109 (v4, 16 Nov 2026).** [PREPRINT; v1 was titled "Generative agent simulations of 1,000 people"] — [arXiv:2411.10109](https://arxiv.org/abs/2411.10109)
  - *Design and sample:* 2-hour AI-conducted interviews with 1,052 U.S. adults.
  - *Raw General Social Survey accuracy:* **demographics-only simulation 60.4%; interview-based 69.4%; survey-based 69.3%; combined 72.8%**. Participants' own two-week test-retest agreement was 79.5%.
  - Surveys and interviews **did not improve prediction of incentivised economic choices**, and models stayed "hyper-rational".
  - *Bears on:* (4). Demographic personas, the typical student approach, are the weakest option.
  - *Note:* the widely quoted "85%" is a *normalised* figure from v1 relative to test-retest, not raw accuracy ([secondary explanation](https://hai.stanford.edu/policy/simulating-human-behavior-with-ai-agents)).

**C. Threat to "real" online samples**

- **Westwood, S. J. (2025). The potential existential threat of large language models to online survey research. *PNAS, 122*(47), e2518075122.** [PR] — [doi:10.1073/pnas.2518075122](https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.2518075122)
  - An autonomous synthetic respondent **passed 99.8% of 6,000 standard attention-check trials** and evaded logic puzzles and "reverse shibboleth" items.

### Inferences
- **Proposal (4) is strongly supported.** Peer-reviewed critiques (Bisbee; Boelaert; Dominguez-Olmedo; Wang; von der Heyde) converge on the same failure pattern: plausible averages, too little variance, biased subgroups, instability across prompts and time. A student who replaces 100 respondents with ChatGPT personas will get data that looks fine but supports invalid inferences.
- **Nuance for business students.** Marketing journals (Li; Brand; Arora; Ashokkumar in Nature) show real value for **pilot testing a questionnaire, generating hypotheses, anticipating the direction of effects, or enriching personas**. A balanced learning objective is therefore "know when synthetic data is acceptable (upstream, exploratory, with human validation) and when it is not (as the evidence base for conclusions)". Sarstedt et al. (2024) give a ready-made framework.
- **Dutch context.** von der Heyde et al. (Germany) suggest that validation on U.S. data does not transfer, which makes Dutch-language synthetic respondents even less trustworthy without local validation.
- **Data-integrity angle.** Westwood (2025) adds a second learning goal for (4): synthetic data may enter a "real" sample. Students should learn bot-screening limits and prefer controlled samples.

### Gaps
- No peer-reviewed study validates synthetic respondents on **Dutch** consumers or B2B or organisational respondents, which are typical in hbo business theses.
- No evidence was found on **synthetic interviewees** (LLM-simulated qualitative interviews) compared with real interviews, beyond Arora et al. (2025) and the Park et al. preprint.
- The following were located but their content was not checked, so they are not used: Lin, Z. (2025), "Six fallacies in substituting large language models for human participants" (PsyArXiv, [doi:10.31234/osf.io/uqxcb_v2](https://doi.org/10.31234/osf.io/uqxcb_v2)); Kozlowski & Evans (2025), "Simulating subjects" (SocArXiv, [doi:10.31235/osf.io/vp3j2_v3](https://doi.org/10.31235/osf.io/vp3j2_v3)); Dillion et al. (2023), *Trends in Cognitive Sciences, 27*(7), 597–600 ([doi:10.1016/j.tics.2023.04.008](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.tics.2023.04.008)); and an AAPOR 2026 conference benchmark.
- The exact correlation reported in the Nature version of Ashokkumar et al. was not verified.

## Key question 4: Does seeding AI with hand-picked high-quality sources (exemplars, few-shot prompting, citation chasing) improve the relevance and quality of literature search?

### Takeaway
There is **no direct study** of the proposed didactic move ("find 3 good sources, give them to AI as examples, ask for more of the same quality"), and no independent peer-reviewed evaluation of ResearchRabbit or Connected Papers. Adjacent evidence is supportive in principle. Professional searchers use **"seed studies"** to build and test queries. **Citation tracking** from known relevant records adds value in about 96% of methodological studies. Including a good example in a prompt improved ChatGPT's Boolean queries. Few-shot examples improve LLM coding and synthetic data. The nuances are that LLM query generation is **not reproducible** across runs, that example-based prompting tends to trade recall for precision, and that AI tools have low sensitivity, so the seed set can narrow the search instead of broadening it.

### Cited Findings

**A. Seed studies and citation chasing in information retrieval (the professional analogue of proposal 1)**

- **Wang, S., Scells, H., Clark, J., Koopman, B., & Zuccon, G. (2022). From little things big things grow: A collection with seed studies for medical systematic review literature search. In *Proceedings of the 45th International ACM SIGIR Conference* (pp. 3176–3186). ACM.** [PR; foundational] — [doi:10.1145/3477495.3531748](https://doi.org/10.1145/3477495.3531748)
  - Information specialists use **"a set of exemplar documents, called 'seed studies'"** before formulating queries, both to build queries and to verify that they work.
  - "Pseudo" seed studies (taken retrospectively from included studies) are **not representative of real seeds**.
  - Seeds can guide automatic query formulation and retrieval models.
  - The full text ([arXiv:2204.03096](https://arxiv.org/abs/2204.03096), as quoted in search results) reports that **queries formulated from real seed studies are more effective (precision and recall) than pseudo-seeds for the "objective" method**. For the "conceptual" method, real seeds gave lower precision but higher recall.
  - *Bears on:* (1).
- **Wang, S., Scells, H., Koopman, B., & Zuccon, G. (2023). Can ChatGPT write a good Boolean query for systematic review literature search? In *Proceedings of the 46th International ACM SIGIR Conference* (pp. 1426–1436). ACM.** [PR] — [doi:10.1145/3539618.3591703](https://doi.org/10.1145/3539618.3591703)
  - ChatGPT generates **highly precise queries but trades off recall**, which suits rapid reviews.
  - The arXiv version reports that **prompts that include a high-quality example significantly outperform prompts without one**, and that a topically related example raises precision but lowers recall ([arXiv:2302.03495](https://arxiv.org/abs/2302.03495), as quoted in search results).
  - *Bears on:* (1).
- **Staudinger, M., Kusa, W., Piroi, F., Lipani, A., & Hanbury, A. (2024). A reproducibility and generalizability study of large language models for query generation. In *Proceedings of the 2024 Annual International ACM SIGIR Conference on Research and Development in Information Retrieval in the Asia Pacific Region (SIGIR-AP '24)* (pp. 186–196). ACM.** [PR] — [doi:10.1145/3673791.3698432](https://doi.org/10.1145/3673791.3698432); [arXiv:2411.14914](https://arxiv.org/abs/2411.14914)
  - Reproduced Wang et al. (2023) and Alaniz et al. with ChatGPT, Mistral and Zephyr.
  - Results were **"not reproducible nor reliable"** across reruns, with high variance, and there was **no clear trend toward recall or precision**. Recall was better on the seed-based dataset.
  - *Bears on:* (1), (3).
- **Hirt, J., Nordhausen, T., Appenzeller-Herzog, C., & Ewald, H. (2023). Citation tracking for systematic literature searching: A scoping review. *Research Synthesis Methods, 14*(3), 563–579.** [PR] — [doi:10.1002/jrsm.1635](https://doi.org/10.1002/jrsm.1635)
  - 47 methodological studies (1985–2021).
  - **96% found added value of citation tracking** (forward and backward from known relevant records).
  - Benefit depends on factors that could not be assessed with certainty.
  - *Bears on:* (1).
- **Hirt, J., Nordhausen, T., Fuerst, T., Ewald, H., & Appenzeller-Herzog, C. (2024). Guidance on terminology, application, and reporting of citation searching: The TARCiS statement. *BMJ, 385*, e078384.** [PR] — [doi:10.1136/bmj-2023-078384](https://doi.org/10.1136/bmj-2023-078384)
  - A 4-round Delphi with 27 experts produced **10 recommendations** on when and how to do and report citation searching.
  - A ready-made reporting standard for students' "snowballing" from seed sources.
  - *Bears on:* (1).
- **Haddaway, N. R., Grainger, M. J., & Gray, C. T. (2022). Citationchaser: A tool for transparent and efficient forward and backward citation chasing in systematic searching. *Research Synthesis Methods, 13*(4), 533–545.** [PR; tool paper] — [doi:10.1002/jrsm.1563](https://doi.org/10.1002/jrsm.1563)
  - A free R/Shiny tool that chases citations "from a starting set of articles".
  - Citation chasing finds **"terminologically disconnected but semantically linked"** studies that keyword searches miss.
  - A transparent, non-generative alternative to ResearchRabbit-type tools.
  - *Bears on:* (1), (3).
- **van de Schoot, R., de Bruin, J., Schram, R., Zahedi, P., de Boer, J., Weijdema, F., Kramer, B., Huijts, M., Hoogerwerf, M., Ferdinands, G., Harkema, A., Willemsen, J., Ma, Y., Fang, Q., Hindriks, S., Tummers, L., & Oberski, D. L. (2021). An open source machine learning framework for efficient and transparent systematic reviews. *Nature Machine Intelligence, 3*(2), 125–133.** [PR; foundational; **Dutch** (Utrecht University), ASReview] — [doi:10.1038/s42256-020-00287-7](https://doi.org/10.1038/s42256-020-00287-7)
  - Active learning "can yield far more efficient reviewing than manual reviewing while providing high quality" (simulation studies).
  - The model learns from records the human labels as relevant, a screening analogue of "seed with good examples".
  - The detail that ASReview requires user-supplied prior relevant records comes from the tool documentation and was not checked in this session (UNVERIFIED).
  - *Bears on:* (1), (3).

**B. Exemplars (few-shot) in neighbouring tasks**

- **Balt et al. (2025)**, see KQ2: one positive and one negative example per code gave κ = 0.68 on deductive coding. [PR] [doi:10.3389/fpubh.2025.1512537](https://doi.org/10.3389/fpubh.2025.1512537)
- **Arora et al. (2025)**, see KQ3: few-shot learning and RAG **significantly improved** the quality of synthetic quantitative data. [PR] [doi:10.1177/00222429241276529](https://doi.org/10.1177/00222429241276529)
- **Lau & Golder (2025)**, see KQ1: Elicit precision 41.8% but sensitivity 39.5%. AI search returns relevant papers but misses most of them, so it works as an adjunct to a human-built seed set, not as the main search. [PR] [doi:10.1002/cesm.70050](https://doi.org/10.1002/cesm.70050)
- **Bernard et al. (2025)**, see KQ1: Elicit found 3 relevant studies the traditional search missed but missed 17 that the traditional search found. [PR] [doi:10.1186/s12874-025-02528-y](https://doi.org/10.1186/s12874-025-02528-y)

**C. Citation-mapping tools (ResearchRabbit, Connected Papers, Litmaps)**

- **No independent peer-reviewed relevance evaluation was found.**
- Vendor blogs report "blind tests" with only 3 seed papers, in which Connected Papers scored about 1.77/2 for relevance and ResearchRabbit about 1.25/2 ([Paperguide, VENDOR](https://paperguide.ai/blog/connected-papers-vs-researchrabbit/)). Not usable as evidence.
- Practitioner guides advise a **varied seed set**, because seeds sharing one theory, country or research group narrow the recommendations ([IntuitionLabs guide, GREY](https://intuitionlabs.ai/articles/ai-literature-mapping-tools-guide)). This is a rule of thumb, not a tested finding.
- *Mechanism (tool documentation level, per the same guides):* Connected Papers builds a graph from one seed using co-citation and bibliographic coupling; ResearchRabbit accepts many seeds.

### Inferences
- **Proposal (1) matches established professional practice.** Seed studies (Wang 2022), citation tracking (Hirt 2023; TARCiS 2024) and active learning from labelled examples (ASReview) all start from human-selected relevant records. Asking students to first find 3 good sources and then expand from them, by citation chasing or AI "find similar", is a defensible, literature-backed design. It is also *reportable* with TARCiS.
- **Nuances to build in:**
  - Example-based prompting raises precision but can **lower recall** and narrow the search. The echo-chamber effect (Sharma et al. 2024) suggests AI will tend to return "more of the same", which is a risk for scope.
  - LLM query generation is **not reproducible** (Staudinger et al.).
  - AI results still contain fabricated or misattributed items (KQ1), so "check the result" in proposal (1) has to include existence *and* relevance checks.
  - Citation-graph tools (citationchaser, Connected Papers) do not generate text and therefore do not fabricate. They are a safer first step than chat-based "find similar" prompts.
- **What to ask students to evaluate** (this list is my inference): recall (what the AI missed that a database search finds), precision, diversity of the AI-suggested set relative to the seeds, and the share of non-existent or irrelevant items.

### Gaps
- **No empirical study** found on students, or on researchers in management or business, seeding an LLM or AI search tool with a small hand-picked exemplar set and measuring the quality or relevance of what it returns. This is a genuine evidence gap, and potentially a HAN practice-based research opportunity.
- No independent peer-reviewed comparison of ResearchRabbit, Connected Papers, Litmaps, Semantic Scholar recommendations or Elicit "find similar" on relevance.
- Seed-study evidence comes almost entirely from medical systematic reviews, where Boolean searching in PubMed is standard. Transfer to business databases (EBSCO Business Source, ABI/INFORM, Google Scholar) is untested.

## Key question 5: What is known about higher-education students using AI for literature reviews and research projects, including effects on source quality, learning and academic integrity?

### Takeaway
Student use is now near-universal. 88% of UK undergraduates used GenAI for assessments in 2025, and 64.7% of German economics and business students use chatbots for literature searching, more than library catalogues (58.4%) or business databases (41%). Students are aware of hallucinations (92.4%) but struggle to judge relevance (53.6%) and want training. Controlled experiments consistently show that AI support **lowers effort and raises immediate output, but reduces depth of learning and reasoning quality**, and that removing the AI afterwards can leave students *worse off* than peers who never used it, unless guardrails are in place. No controlled study was found that directly measures the quality of sources selected by students with and without AI.

### Cited Findings

**A. Usage and attitudes (grey literature, large samples)**

- **Siegfried, D., & Flieger, E. (2026). *Zwischen Google Scholar und ChatGPT – Wie Wirtschaftsstudierende heute recherchieren*. ZBW – Leibniz Information Centre for Economics.** [GREY; report on EconStor, handle 10419/342604, handle not independently resolved] — summary: [ZBW MediaTalk, 14 Oct 2026](https://www.zbw-mediatalk.eu/2026/10/between-google-scholar-and-chatgpt-how-students-conduct-research-in-2026/)
  - *Design and sample:* online survey of **908 economics and business students** at German universities, March–May 2026.
  - *Search channels:* **64.7% use chatbots or general AI tools (ChatGPT, Gemini) for literature search sometimes or often**, against 58.4% for library catalogues and 41% for business databases. 91.3% use Google or Google Scholar. Specialised AI tools (Elicit, Consensus, SciSpace) play a much smaller role.
  - *Database awareness:* about half do not know Factiva, Nexis Uni, ProQuest One Business or RePEc.
  - *Risk awareness:* **92.4% recognise the risk of hallucinated results**, and 69.8% worry about over-dependence.
  - *Difficulties:* **53.6% struggle to judge relevance**, and 62.5% find full-text access difficult.
  - *Training wanted:* identifying trustworthy sources 89.6%; using AI for literature search 81.3%.
  - *Bears on:* (1), (3), (5). This is the closest population to HAN business students.
- **Freeman, J. (2025). *Student Generative AI Survey 2025* (HEPI Policy Note 61). Higher Education Policy Institute / Kortext.** [GREY] — [HEPI page](https://www.hepi.ac.uk/2025/02/26/student-generative-ai-survey-2025/); [PDF](https://www.hepi.ac.uk/wp-content/uploads/2025/02/HEPI-Policy-Note-61.pdf)
  - *Sample:* 1,041 UK full-time undergraduates (Savanta).
  - **88% used GenAI for assessments (53% in 2024).** The most common uses were explaining concepts, **summarising articles and suggesting research ideas**.
  - **18% included AI-generated and edited text in assessments.**
  - A 2026 edition reports 95% overall use (UNVERIFIED detail).
  - *Bears on:* (5), and academic integrity.

**B. Experiments: AI vs traditional search and learning depth**

- **Stadler, M., Bannert, M., & Sailer, M. (2024). Cognitive ease at a cost: LLMs reduce mental effort but compromise depth in student scientific inquiry. *Computers in Human Behavior, 160*, 108386.** [PR] — [doi:10.1016/j.chb.2024.108386](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.chb.2024.108386)
  - *Design and sample:* **91 university students** randomly assigned to **ChatGPT-3.5 or Google** to research a socio-scientific issue (nanoparticles in sunscreen) and give justified recommendations.
  - The LLM group had **significantly lower cognitive load but lower-quality reasoning and argumentation.**
  - Homogeneity of the recommendations did not differ.
  - *Bears on:* (1), (3), (5). The most direct evidence on student information-gathering with AI.
- **Melumad, S., & Yun, J. H. (2025). Experimental evidence of the effects of large language models versus web search on depth of learning. *PNAS Nexus, 4*(10), pgaf316.** [PR] — [doi:10.1093/pnasnexus/pgaf316](https://doi.org/10.1093/pnasnexus/pgaf316)
  - **7 online and lab experiments (n = 10,462).**
  - Learning from LLM syntheses rather than web links led to **shallower knowledge** and to advice that was **sparser, less original and less likely to be adopted** by recipients. This held **even when the LLM output included real-time web links.**
  - Mechanism: users no longer actively discover and synthesise sources themselves.
  - *Bears on:* (1): the case for finding sources yourself first.
- **Bastani, H., Bastani, O., Sungu, A., Ge, H., Kabakcı, Ö., & Mariman, R. (2025). Generative AI without guardrails can harm learning: Evidence from high school mathematics. *PNAS, 122*(26), e2422633122.** [PR; field experiment, secondary school, not HE]
  - [doi:10.1073/pnas.2422633122](https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.2422633122)
  - *Sample:* about 1,000 students.
  - *During practice:* GPT-4 access **improved grades by 48% (standard interface) and 127% (tutor with safeguards)**.
  - *After access was removed:* standard-interface students scored **17% lower than students who never had access**. Guardrails largely mitigated the harm.
  - *Bears on:* (1), (2): the logic of doing it yourself first.
- **Fan, Y., Tang, L., Le, H., Shen, K., Tan, S., Zhao, Y., Shen, Y., Li, X., & Gašević, D. (2025). Beware of metacognitive laziness: Effects of generative artificial intelligence on learning motivation, processes, and performance. *British Journal of Educational Technology, 56*(2), 489–530.** [PR] — [doi:10.1111/bjet.13544](https://doi.org/10.1111/bjet.13544)
  - *Design and sample:* randomised experiment with **117 university students** on a writing task; conditions were ChatGPT, a human expert, writing analytics, or no support.
  - The ChatGPT group **improved essay scores most, but knowledge gain and transfer did not differ**.
  - Self-regulated learning processes differed, which the authors call "metacognitive laziness".
  - *Bears on:* (2), (5).
- **Lee, H.-P., Sarkar, A., Tankelevitch, L., Drosos, I., Rintel, S., Banks, R., & Wilson, N. (2025). The impact of generative AI on critical thinking: Self-reported reductions in cognitive effort and confidence effects from a survey of knowledge workers. In *Proceedings of the 2025 CHI Conference on Human Factors in Computing Systems* (pp. 1–22). ACM.** [PR; Microsoft Research]
  - [doi:10.1145/3706598.3713778](https://doi.org/10.1145/3706598.3713778)
  - *Sample:* 319 knowledge workers sharing 936 examples of GenAI use.
  - **Higher confidence in GenAI was associated with less critical thinking; higher self-confidence with more.**
  - GenAI shifts critical thinking toward **information verification, response integration and task stewardship**.
  - *Bears on:* (5). Verification becomes the core skill.
- **Kosmyna, N., et al. (2025). Your brain on ChatGPT: Accumulation of cognitive debt when using an AI assistant for essay writing task. arXiv:2506.08872.** [PREPRINT; MIT Media Lab; full author list UNVERIFIED] — [arXiv:2506.08872](https://arxiv.org/abs/2506.08872)
  - *Sample:* 54 participants in 3 groups (LLM, search engine, brain only); EEG.
  - The LLM group had the weakest neural connectivity, the lowest sense of ownership, and **0 of 18 could correctly quote their own essay** in session 1.
  - *Limitations:* small sample, not peer-reviewed. Press coverage overstates the results.
  - *Bears on:* (1), (2) (weak evidence).

**C. Science-level warning**

- **Messeri, L., & Crockett, M. J. (2024). Artificial intelligence and illusions of understanding in scientific research. *Nature, 627*(8002), 49–58.** [PR; perspective] — [doi:10.1038/s41586-024-07146-0](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41586-024-07146-0)
  - AI tools can exploit cognitive limitations, producing **"illusions of understanding in which we believe we understand more about the world than we actually do"**.
  - They also risk "scientific monocultures" in which "we produce more but understand less".
  - *Bears on:* (4), (5), and the overall rationale.

### Inferences
- **The "human first, then AI" design in proposals (1) and (2) is supported by the learning-science evidence.** Bastani et al., Fan et al., Stadler et al. and Melumad & Yun all find that unstructured AI use improves the immediate product while weakening learning, reasoning depth or later independent performance. Requiring students to do the cognitive work first and then compare is a guardrail of the kind that mitigated harm in Bastani et al. This is an inference: none of these studies tested research-skills education in hbo specifically.
- **Proposal (5)** is supported by Lee et al. (verification becomes the critical-thinking task), Spatharioti et al. (users over-rely on wrong LLM answers without noticing) and KQ1 (30–97% unsupported statements even when the references are real). The ZBW survey shows students already *know* about hallucination risk (92.4%) but lack the skills: 53.6% struggle to judge relevance. Awareness is not competence.
- **Proposal (3)** fits a clear gap in business students' search skills. They use chatbots more than library catalogues or business databases, and many do not know key business databases (ZBW 2026). Teaching how AI search works should be paired with teaching discipline databases.
- **Academic integrity.** Fabricated references are a common marker of undeclared AI use, and even expert peer review misses them (GPTZero/NeurIPS). A verification step in which students show that they read each source also works as an integrity safeguard.

### Gaps
- **No controlled study** found that compares the *quality of sources* (peer-reviewed share, relevance, recency, fabrication) in student literature reviews or theses produced with and without AI. The closest is Stadler et al. on reasoning quality.
- No Dutch hbo or wo data were found on how business students use AI for literature research. The ZBW (Germany) and HEPI (UK) surveys are the nearest proxies. SURF or Npuls reports may exist but were not checked.
- Gerlich (2025), *Societies, 15*(1), 6 ([doi:10.3390/soc15010006](https://doi.org/10.3390/soc15010006)), on cognitive offloading and critical thinking, was verified in Crossref but its findings were not reviewed in this session.
- Most learning experiments use writing or maths tasks, not research tasks such as source selection, coding or survey analysis.
- **Survey and quantitative analysis by students with AI** (for example ChatGPT data analysis, or SPSS/R code generation) was in scope, but no peer-reviewed accuracy studies were located in this pass. This is an open gap for the report writer.
