# International frameworks and evidence (2023–2026) for AI literacy, research skills and AI-resilient assessment in higher-education curricula

Research date: 18 November 2026. These notes support a curriculum memo for a Dutch university of applied sciences (business administration).

**Proposal codes used below**
- **P1**: Research skills in a fixed sequence: first without AI, then with AI, then compare.
- **P2**: Verifying AI output is a standard research skill from year 1.
- **P3**: Oral or in-person explanation of analyses, because AI-assisted products say less about competence.
- **P4**: In the research report, students justify what AI did and how they checked it.
- **P5**: Students keep "the lead" over the whole research process (question, design choices, steering AI, judging the outcome) and own stakeholder contact and communication.

**Evidence-type labels**
- [BINDING]: EU regulation.
- [OFFICIAL FRAMEWORK/GUIDANCE]: intergovernmental bodies, regulators, professional bodies.
- [PEER-REVIEWED]: journal articles or conference papers.
- [WORKING PAPER/PREPRINT]: not yet peer reviewed.
- [OPINION/PRACTITIONER]: blogs, magazine articles, vendor material.

**Verification labels**
- "Verified (Crossref)": the DOI metadata (authors, title, journal, volume, issue, pages, date) were checked against the Crossref API on 18 Nov 2026.
- "Verified (official page)": the details were read on the issuer's own page or PDF.
- UNVERIFIED: the item could not be confirmed against a primary record.

## Q1. What do the main AI-literacy and AI-competency frameworks say for students (UNESCO 2024; EC/OECD AI Literacy Framework; EU AI Act Article 4; DigComp 3.0; AI-related information-literacy frameworks)?

### Takeaway
All the major frameworks are now final as of November 2026: UNESCO (2024), DigComp 3.0 (late 2025) and the OECD/EC AI Literacy Framework (June 2026). All are non-binding, and the UNESCO and OECD/EC frameworks are written for school education, not higher education. Their common message is human judgement and agency over AI and a critical, evaluative stance towards AI outputs. That gives framework-level support for P2 and P5 and indirect support for P1. The only binding instrument, Article 4 of the EU AI Act, places a duty on the institution towards its *staff and persons acting on its behalf*. It does not create learning outcomes for students. Since 27 July 2026 it has been softened from "ensure a sufficient level" to "take measures to support".

### Inferences
- **Article 4 is not a student-curriculum mandate.** Its duty runs to the institution as a *deployer*, towards "staff and other persons ... on their behalf". Students are normally not acting on the institution's behalf. Lecturers and assessors who use AI tools (for example in grading or feedback) clearly are. A memo should therefore present student AI literacy as an *educational* choice supported by UNESCO, OECD/EC and DigComp, not as a legal obligation. It can present staff AI literacy as a legal duty, now a duty of effort with documented measures.
- **Annex III 3(b) is a design constraint** for any plan to use AI to assess P3 orals or P4 justifications. Using AI to "evaluate learning outcomes" would bring in high-risk obligations, likely from 2 December 2027.
- **The frameworks share a three-part logic.** They combine (a) knowing when not to use AI, (b) critically evaluating outputs and (c) human judgement and accountability. That maps well onto P1, P2 and P5, although none of them prescribes the specific "without → with → compare" sequence.
- **Higher-education framework gap.** The two most-cited frameworks (UNESCO, OECD/EC) target school education. For a university of applied sciences, DigComp 3.0 (citizens) and discipline frameworks (information literacy, assessment) are more directly applicable. The memo should present UNESCO and OECD/EC as describing the *entry level* of incoming students.

## Q2. What assessment frameworks for the AI era exist (AI Assessment Scale 2024/2025, Sydney two-lane approach, TEQSA 2025, Jisc/QAA, orals and process-based assessment), and what evidence supports them?

### Takeaway
The field has moved from rules about permitted AI use, such as the 2024 AIAS and traffic-light labels, to *structural* redesign. That means program-level "secure" checkpoints (Sydney lane 1; TEQSA 2025 pathways) and performative or oral formats. There is strong empirical evidence that unsupervised written products no longer show competence:
- 94% of AI-written exam submissions went undetected and outscored real students.
- Markers could not distinguish GenAI-assisted work, even on authentic tasks.
- GenAI masked performance differences in a business-school exam.

This strongly supports P3. Direct validity evidence for oral formats in the AI era is still thin (pilots and practice reports).

**Critique of rule-based approaches, including AIAS-type scales**: [PEER-REVIEWED, conceptual]
- **Citation**: Corbin, T., Dawson, P., & Liu, D. (2025). Talk is cheap: Why structural assessment changes are needed for a time of GenAI. *Assessment & Evaluation in Higher Education, 50*(7), 1087–1097. https://doi.org/10.1080/02602938.2025.2503964 — Verified (Crossref)
- **Content**:
  - Distinguishes "discursive changes to assessment (modifications relying solely on instructions students remain free to ignore)" from "structural changes (modifications that reshape the underlying mechanics of assessment tasks themselves)".
  - Argues that traffic-light approaches create an "enforcement illusion".
  - Calls for "structural assessment redesign that builds validity into assessment architecture rather than attempting to impose it through unenforceable rules".
  - Source: [abstract via DOI](https://doi.org/10.1080/02602938.2025.2503964)
- **Bears on**: supports P3 (a structural check). It **challenges** P4 if the AI justification is only an unenforceable statement, and challenges relying on AIAS-type labels alone.

**Validity rather than cheating**: [PEER-REVIEWED, conceptual]
- **Citation**: Dawson, P., Bearman, M., Dollinger, M., & Boud, D. (2024). Validity matters more than cheating. *Assessment & Evaluation in Higher Education, 49*(7), 1005–1016. https://doi.org/10.1080/02602938.2024.2386662 — Verified (Crossref)
- **Content**: reframes cheating as part of assessment validity. Warns that anti-cheating measures can harm validity (for example through inclusion problems). ## Q3. What guidance exists on students disclosing and justifying AI use in research reports (AI-use statements, APA guidance, acknowledgement templates), and is there evidence that such statements work?

### Takeaway
Official guidance now exists. APA's *AI Contribution Statement Guide* (updated September 2026) sets minimum components: dates, prompts, sections affected, and the ideas generated and how they were incorporated. It also suggests placing the statement in the Method section and keeping an "AI contribution journal". APA's minimum components do **not** include *how the student verified* AI output, which P4 adds. The evidence on whether declarations work is sobering:
- At one business school, 74% of students did not complete a mandatory AI declaration, mostly out of fear and unclear rules.
- 13 experiments show that people who disclose AI use are trusted less, even when disclosure is mandatory.
- Conceptually, declarations are "discursive" measures with an "enforcement illusion".

### Cited Findings

**APA AI Contribution Statement Guide (2026)**: [OFFICIAL GUIDANCE (style authority)]
- **Citation**: American Psychological Association. (2026). *AI contribution statement guide* [Instructional aid; last updated 1 September 2026]. https://apastyle.apa.org/instructional-aids/ai-contribution-statement.pdf — Verified (official PDF, text extracted)

## Q4. What do recent studies or frameworks say about "human-in-the-loop" or "human agency" competences for directing, monitoring and evaluating AI work, especially in business or management education?

### Takeaway
Peer-reviewed evidence from knowledge work and education points the same way:
- With GenAI, critical thinking shifts towards "information verification, response integration, and task stewardship" (P2, P5).
- Over-reliance outside AI's competence frontier lowers correctness by about 19 percentage points among consultants (P2, P5).
- Unguarded AI use during practice harms later independent performance (P1).

Management-education journals give business-specific evidence (AMLE 2026 on exam performance convergence; a JME 2025 assignment that pairs an AI draft with real stakeholder interviews). They also offer critical voices (British Journal of Management). AACSB offers only opinion pieces, not standards. I found no EFMD guidance.

### Inferences
- **P5 is supported by evidence and by frameworks.**
  - The empirical case: unsupervised reliance degrades correctness on ill-defined, judgement-heavy tasks (Dell'Acqua; Bergenholtz), and critical thinking moves to verification and stewardship (Lee et al.).
  - The framework case: UNESCO, OECD/EC and DigComp all frame human judgement and control as non-delegable.
  - Business-research problems from commissioning organisations are typically "outside the frontier": ill-defined, context-specific and stakeholder-dependent.
- **P1 is supported indirectly but consistently.** Bastani (harm after unguarded practice), Fan (better product, no better learning), Bergenholtz (low performers bypass analysis) and Lee (self-confidence predicts critical thinking) all suggest that students need independent competence first, or they cannot judge AI output.
  - No study tested the exact "without AI → with AI → compare" sequence in higher-education research-methods teaching. The memo should present P1 as an evidence-*informed* design choice, not an evidence-*proven* one.
- **Walker & Larson (JME 2025) is the closest published business-education exemplar** of the memo's logic. AI does the draft, students do real stakeholder data collection, and students compare and refine. It can be cited as a model for P1 + P5 in a business programme.
- **Critical management scholarship** (Lindebaum & Fleming 2024; Lindebaum et al. 2026) lets the memo acknowledge a stronger position. Some argue GenAI erodes reflexivity and practical wisdom, which strengthens the case for keeping problem definition and stakeholder interaction human.

### Gaps
- I found no EFMD guidance or standard on AI and human agency competences, and no formal AACSB accreditation standard on AI (only *Insights* opinion pieces, whose details are unverified).
- I found no AMLE or JME article (2024–2026) that specifically defines competences for directing or monitoring *AI agents* (as opposed to chatbots) in student research. The agent-oriented material found is practitioner opinion (AACSB July 2026).
- The Dell'Acqua effect sizes come from secondary summaries and should be checked against the Organization Science article.
- Most educational effect studies are outside higher-education business research courses (Bastani: secondary school; Fan: writing task), which limits transfer.
